Shwedagon Pagoda

The Great Golden Mountain of Myanmar

Rising above the city of Yangon, the Shwedagon Pagoda has stood as Myanmar's most sacred site for longer than most nations have existed. This is its story.

Shwedagon Zedi Daw

Its formal name, Shwedagon Zedi Daw, translates to "The Great Golden Mountain Stupa." The name is fitting. The stupa's brick plinth is layered with genuine gold plates, and the bell-shaped structure above it is covered in gold from base to spire, a tradition of devotion that began in the 15th century when Queen Shin Sawbu donated her own weight in gold to the pagoda. Devotees and monarchs alike have continued the practice ever since, each layer of gold an act of merit as much as an act of maintenance.

  • Height: approximately 99 meters (326 feet), rising to around 112 meters including the crowning umbrella
  • Location: Singuttara Hill, Yangon, Myanmar
  • Status: the most sacred Buddhist stupa in Myanmar

Founded on a Gift of Hair

Legend holds that the Shwedagon Pagoda was founded more than 2,500 years ago, while the Buddha himself was still living, which would make it the oldest Buddhist stupa in the world. Two merchant brothers, Tapussa and Bhallika, are said to have traveled from their home near what is now Yangon to meet the Buddha in India. They offered him alms of honey and rice cakes, and in return he gave them eight strands of his own hair to carry home.

King Okkalapa is said to have enshrined those eight hairs on Singuttara Hill, alongside relics already believed to belong to three Buddhas who came before Gautama. From that founding act, the Shwedagon grew, terrace by terrace, gold layer by gold layer, into the towering monument that shapes Yangon's skyline today.

What the Pagoda Holds

Buddhist tradition holds that the Shwedagon enshrines relics of not one, but four Buddhas, each honored at a shrine facing one of the four cardinal directions.

East

Kakusandha Buddha

Honored with his staff, said to have been enshrined at the site's founding.

South

Konagamana Buddha

Honored with his water filter, one of the pagoda's oldest relics.

West

Kassapa Buddha

Honored with a piece of his robe.

North

Gautama Buddha

Honored with the eight strands of hair that began it all.

Devotees circle the stupa clockwise, passing each shrine in turn, reciting prayers as they go. It's a practice known as circumambulation, a way of walking the wheel of the Buddha's teaching one step at a time.

Half Lion, Wholly Legend

At each of the pagoda's four entrances stand pairs of Chinthe, Myanmar's mythical guardian lions. Lions were never native to Myanmar, so generations of artisans shaped the Chinthe from imagination alone, giving it a flame-like mane, an open, fanged mouth, and a posture built for vigilance rather than rest.

Legend holds that the Chinthe traces back to a princess named Suppadevi, whose son was born of her marriage to a lion. When she and her son fled the lion's den, the abandoned lion fell into a rage that terrorized the countryside. Her son ultimately hunted and killed the lion, and it was only afterward that he learned the truth of his own parentage. Grief-stricken, he built a statue of the lion to guard a temple gate, an act of atonement that echoes in every Chinthe carved since.

Wherever a Chinthe stands today, in pairs at a doorway or a stairway, its purpose is the same: to turn back misfortune and keep sacred ground sacred.

One Nation Among Ten

Myanmar has been a member of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations since 1997. Like the pagoda at its heart, Myanmar's story is one of layered history, deep spiritual tradition, and a golden thread of identity that continues through every generation that adds to it.
